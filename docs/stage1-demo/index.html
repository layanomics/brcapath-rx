What is the C-index?

The C-index tells us how well the model ranks which patients are relatively higher risk versus lower risk within the same cohort. A value of 0.5 is no better than chance; higher values mean better ranking.

The core question

Pick two patients. One survived longer than the other. Did the model assign the longer-survivor a lower risk score than the shorter-survivor? If yes, that pair is concordant. The C-index is the fraction of all comparable pairs that are concordant.

Result: 6 concordant pairs out of 6 possible → C-index = 1.0 (perfect). In real clinical data, some pairs will be mis-ordered. A C-index of 0.729 means roughly 73% of pairs are correctly ordered — meaningfully better than chance (0.5), without claiming perfection.
Important caveat: A higher C-index means the model ranks survival risk better within a cohort. It does not mean the model predicts exact survival times, identifies causally important features, or estimates treatment effects. This is a prognostic ranking tool, not a treatment predictor.

What kind of model is this?

This demo uses a Cox proportional hazards model — a standard, well-understood statistical method for survival analysis used routinely in oncology research.

The model learns how each input feature (age, subtype, stage or NPI) relates to the relative hazard of dying in the training cohort. It does not require specifying a fixed survival distribution — the baseline hazard is estimated from the observed data.

Key formula
Hazard ratio = exp(β·age + β·subtype + …)

S(t) = S0(t) HazardRatio
where S0(t) is the baseline survival estimated from training data.

Why this is Stage 1 only

What Stage 1 answers
  • ✓ Can we build a prognostic survival model from this public cohort data?
  • ✓ Which features (age, subtype, stage, NPI) are associated with relative survival risk?
  • ✓ What does the derived PAM50 subtype contribute to prognostic ranking?
  • ✓ Are the model’s rankings consistent across two independent cohorts?
What Stage 1 does NOT answer
  • ✗ What is the causal effect of any treatment on survival?
  • ✗ Which treatment should a patient receive?
  • ✗ What would survival be under a counterfactual treatment?
  • ✗ Whether any observed treatment association is causal rather than confounded

Treatment confounding — the fact that sicker patients tend to receive different treatments — was identified as a key challenge in this project. Addressing it is a later-stage question, not what this Stage 1 demo answers.

Scientific limitations

On treatment data and causal inference

Treatment fields (chemotherapy, hormone therapy, radiotherapy) are present in both TCGA-BRCA and METABRIC, but they are observational and incomplete. Patients were not randomly assigned to treatments — sicker patients typically received different, often more aggressive, treatment.

This creates treatment confounding: any naive association between treatment and survival mixes the treatment effect with the underlying prognosis of the patients who received that treatment. You cannot separate the two from observational data without additional causal methods.

Estimating the causal effect of treatment — rather than its observational association — is a later-stage question in the BRCAPath-Rx project. This Stage 1 demo does not address it and does not include treatment fields in any model.

Meeting-safe description of this demo

“This is a cohort-specific Stage 1 prognostic demo. It uses a Cox proportional hazards model to rank relative survival risk within two independent breast cancer cohorts using age, clinical, and molecular subtype features. It does not estimate treatment effects, does not adjust for treatment confounding, and does not recommend treatment.”
