Confounding in plain language

Simple analogy

If the sicker students all get extra tutoring, a raw grade comparison can make the tutoring group look worse. That does not mean tutoring hurt them. It can simply mean the tutoring group started off at a disadvantage.

Breast-cancer version

In real treatment data, patients with higher-risk tumors often receive more treatment. If we compare survival without first making the groups more alike, we can end up measuring baseline risk instead of treatment.

Illustration

A toy picture of the problem

Higher-risk patients are much more likely to receive treatment, and higher-risk patients also have worse survival. If we ignore that starting difference, treatment can falsely look harmful.

What changed

  • Raw imbalance was large. The treated group started with worse measured prognosis.
  • Balance improved after weighting. The before/after diagnostics show the measured gap shrank sharply.
  • The treatment estimate moved a lot. That tells us the naive comparison was unstable and confounded.

What did not change

  • We still do not have randomization.
  • We still lack comorbidity, adherence, exact endocrine regimen, and treatment-era timing.
  • So the adjusted estimate is still provisional, not a treatment recommendation.

How this fits BRCAPath-Rx Stage 2

Stage 1 was prognosis only. This Stage 2 prototype is the first real treatment-confounding track: one explicit treatment question, one cohort, one subgroup, one adjustment method, and diagnostics that show what got better and what still blocks a stronger causal claim.
