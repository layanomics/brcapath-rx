Concrete example

Imagine all possible pairs of patients where one survived longer than the other. The C-index is the fraction of those pairs where the model correctly assigns the higher risk score to the patient who died sooner. A value of 0.729 means the TCGA model correctly ranks ~73 out of every 100 comparable pairs.

Reference benchmarks

  • 0.50 — Random (coin flip). No predictive value.
  • 0.60–0.70 — Moderate discrimination. Useful but imprecise.
  • 0.70–0.80 — Good discrimination. Clinically meaningful ranking.
  • >0.80 — Excellent discrimination. Rarely achieved in survival data.
The C-index measures ranking accuracy — how well the model orders patients by risk. It does not measure calibration (whether predicted probabilities are numerically accurate). Both properties matter for clinical use; this Stage 1 demo addresses ranking only.

How It Works

Prediction pipeline

  1. 1

    Load

    The page fetches ./demo_predictions.json once on startup. This file contains fitted Cox coefficients, centering means, and the Breslow baseline survival function — all pre-exported from Python.

  2. 2

    Select inputs

    The user chooses a cohort (TCGA-BRCA or METABRIC) and adjusts patient-level features. TCGA uses age, AJCC stage, and PAM50 subtype; METABRIC uses age, NPI, and derived PAM50 subtype. The available controls adapt to whichever cohort is selected.

  3. 3

    Compute linear predictor

    JavaScript computes lp = ∑(coefficient × feature value), then centers it by subtracting the training-mean LP: lpc = lp − ∑(coefficient × training mean). This centers the survival curve on the average patient in that cohort.

  4. 4

    Predict and display

    Predicted survival at time t is S0(t)exp(lpc), where S0 is the Breslow baseline. The chart samples ~50 evenly-spaced time points and renders the curve live. No server is involved after the initial JSON fetch.

Why treatment is not modelled

Both TCGA-BRCA and METABRIC include treatment records, but these fields are observational, incomplete, and subject to substantial confounding — patients who received more aggressive treatment were often those with more advanced disease. Adjusting naively for treatment in a Cox model would produce biased estimates and would not answer the question "does this treatment work?" Treatment-effect estimation requires dedicated causal methodology (e.g., propensity-score weighting or instrumental variables) and is a later-stage project objective.
